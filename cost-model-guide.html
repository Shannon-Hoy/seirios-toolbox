Survey Vessel Cost Model, User Guide

Seirios Solutions · Toolbox · Overview, walkthrough, and the benchmark comparison method

What this tool is

The Survey Vessel Cost Model prices a mapping campaign per square kilometer for any vessel. It takes campaign time from the Endurance Planner, fuel from the vessel's own burn curve, labor and consumables from editable cost lines with low and high rate scenarios, and produces total campaign cost, cost per day, cost per square kilometer on two bases, and a benchmark comparison chart. Everything runs in the browser; nothing you enter is uploaded anywhere. Every dollar figure is only as good as the rates you type into it, so treat the defaults as structure, not truth.

The intended workflow

Planner first, cost model second. Draw or import the survey in the Endurance Planner, press Send to Cost Model there, and load the scenario file in this tool's first card. The planner owns time: its mapping days come from calibrated sonar swath curves, polygon bathymetry, line turns, and deployment routing, and this tool deliberately has no depth model of its own to disagree with it. Everything can also be keyed in by hand for quick estimates.

Quick start

Load a scenario (or accept the defaults), open Campaign Parameters and check the day counts, skim the cost lines and replace any rate you know better, then read the Results card. The two hero numbers are cost per km² on the campaign and survey-only bases. Download the .xlsx when you want the work shown: quantities and totals are live formulas referencing the parameters.

Start Here: importing a scenario

Loading a scenario has two routes. Pressing Send to Cost Model in the planner saves a file called scenario_cost_model.json and copies the same text to the clipboard, so in this tool either press Import scenario file and choose that file, or paste the text into the box and press Apply pasted JSON. A four step orientation sits at the top of the same card for anyone opening the tool cold, and every parameter label carries a question mark; click it and a short explanation opens beneath the field.

The import accepts the planner's scenario JSON (schema seirios.costmodel.scenario.v1) by file or paste. It populates area, distance, speeds, mapping and transit days (as overrides), port and weather days, calibrated depth bands, the rotation delta, and the parity knobs: XBTs per mapping day and the post-processing ratios travel with the scenario, so a saved comparison reloads completely. Weather margin days fold into port days so day-counted costs include them; the small distortion is that those days burn in-port fuel in the model rather than at-sea hotel load.

Applying a scenario confirms itself. A panel appears under the import buttons naming the source and listing exactly what the file set: survey area, mapping and transit days, port days, distance, speeds, endurance, deployments, depth bands, the rotation premium and the survey figure. Beneath that sits a live list headed "still yours to check", covering everything the planner cannot supply: that all rates are assumptions, the current XBTs per mapping day against the four to six hour interval contracted surveys often require, the processing hours and what multiple of acquisition time they represent, the port call count against the deployment count and the crew trips they imply, and a warning when the labour lines add up to more people than the persons aboard figure. The Vessel Profile and Campaign Parameters cards also carry a small tag in their headers while collapsed, so it is obvious that their contents came from a scenario rather than sitting at defaults.

Two behaviors worth knowing. First, importing freezes the steamed transit distance, so changing transit speed afterward does not silently move transit fuel; untick the transit days override to thaw it. Second, Copy current scenario JSON round-trips the full setup, which is how to save a named case such as a benchmark parity run.

Vessel Profile

The profile is fuel and speed only: survey and transit speeds, hotel loads, and the fuel curve in liters per nautical mile by speed and hotel load. Burn rates interpolate bilinearly between table cells. The default curve is the Ocean Mapper's with 5% wave resistance assumed; edit any cell or save and load whole profiles as JSON to use the tool for another vessel. Survey productivity deliberately does not live here; it lives in the Endurance Planner.

Campaign Parameters

Mapping days is a plain input, populated by the scenario or typed. Transit days calculate from distance and transit speed unless overridden. Port days, persons on board, endurance, deployments, port calls, XBTs per mapping day, the processing hours per mapping day, and the processing done underway percentage all live here. Deployments and port calls arrive from a planner scenario and can be typed over; deployments drive crew rotation travel (one round trip per person per deployment) and port calls drive berth and agency fees, with each deployment starting and ending alongside and consecutive deployments from one port sharing the call between them. The computed fields carry a small tag reading computed, entered, or from planner, and the tick box beside transit days and persons on board switches between the model's own figure and one you type. Contingency and overhead are toggleable rows at the foot of the Cost Lines card; in the workbook they become a percentage and a Yes/No switch on the Parameters sheet, so the exported total responds when either is changed. The hint line tracks total days against the endurance limit and turns red when a campaign needs splitting into deployments; split it in the planner, not by hand.

Cost Lines

Each line has a driver (what the quantity scales with: total days, mapping days, mapping plus transit days, persons, fuel formulas, lumps, or a manual quantity), a unit, low and high rates, and computed totals. Labor lines also carry low and high crew counts; Mapping Support defaults to 2 to 3 people covering the 24 hour watch, aboard or ashore in any mix. Add lines with the section buttons, remove them with the cross.

Labour lines carry an aboard or shore selector beside their basis, and it does real work. Lines marked aboard count toward persons on board, so food and crew rotation travel scale with them; shore lines do not. Persons on board is therefore computed from the labour lines marked aboard, with an override box for anyone riding along without a labour line such as a client representative. The defaults put the captain and mate aboard and the mapping support and processing ashore, which is the arrangement a highly automated vessel with a two person crew implies; move the support aboard and the complement, the food and the travel all follow. The print report names which lines were marked shore based, since that assumption changes the answer materially.

Two checkboxes control scope. On includes or excludes a line entirely; excluded rows read "off" and dim so nothing is hidden. Svy marks lines counted in the survey-only basis. Vessel capital recovery and sonar amortization are ordinary rows that ship switched off, so the default view is base operating cost; switch them on for a capital-loaded comparison. Contingency and overhead and G&A sit at the bottom of the same card as their own toggleable rows, also off by default, with contingency applied to the cost lines and overhead applied to the cost lines plus contingency.

The two bases

The campaign basis is everything switched On across mapping, transit, and port days. Survey-only drops transit and port from the day-driven lines and excludes lines with Svy unticked; it exists to compare against contracted survey prices, which typically exclude mobilization, demobilization, and transit to the region but include everything else. Check each benchmark's actual basis before trusting the comparison: the KR-OER contract, for example, included port call costs and weather downtime in its price, so for that comparison Port & Agency Fees and Crew Rotation Travel get their Svy boxes ticked and weather rides inside the mapping days.

Post-processing

Processing is priced by the hour at hydrographer rates. Effort equals mapping days times processing hours per mapping day. Because a mapping day is 24 hours of continuous acquisition, the entered hours are also a multiple of acquisition time, and the readout shows that multiple: the defaults of 12 and 36 hours are 0.5 and 1.5 times acquisition, the usual rule of thumb, with the low end for clean deep-water data and the high end for difficult data or heavy deliverables. The processing done underway percentage removes the share the watchstanders absorb between other duties; only the remainder is billable shore time, shown as Billable processing days. Deliverable-grade work (a BAG with an accuracy spec, mosaics, sub-bottom products) argues for ratios near 1.0 to 1.5; a raw-data handoff can sit far lower.

Benchmarks

The benchmarks table takes any platform or contract with a dollars per km² figure and a basis note; checked entries draw as dashed lines on the results chart. Nothing ships enabled. The preset list carries documented reference cases, including the KR-OER-19-01 contracted deep-water survey in two forms, $81 per km² inflation adjusted and $62.46 in the contract's own 2019 dollars, each carrying its full derivation and award citation in the note. The NOAA ship day rate of $17,000 excluding crewing is not a preset, since converting a day rate to dollars per square kilometre requires assuming a productivity figure; it appears instead as the single day-rate comparator beneath the benchmarks table, with its own label, rate and basis note, and the cost per day result quotes it, day rate against day rate. It ships set to the NOAA flat program rate for a day at sea, which excludes ship's crew, so a like-for-like reading should note that the model's own day rate includes any labour lines marked aboard. The report also states plainly that model figures are base cost while benchmark figures are contracted prices or published rates that carry the other party's overhead, margin, risk and scope, so the reader is never left to compare a cost with a price without knowing it. Presets are starting points; edit them freely.

Cost per km² by Depth

This card is first-principles marginal economics, separate from the campaign: the EM124 achievable coverage curve (digitized from the Kongsberg runtime graph, dashed on the right axis) times survey speed, continuous 24 hour mapping, priced at the survey-only day rate. Low and high are the Rate Low and Rate High columns expressed as that day rate; the legend prints both dollar figures. The overlap input trims the swath (pairwise convention: spacing = swath × (1 − overlap), so NOAA-style 20% is entered as 20, never doubled); hover anywhere for the exact cost at that depth, shown first and largest with depth and swath beneath. At 0% overlap the vessel is mowing at full swath with no shared coverage, closer to a mapped transit than a survey; add overlap with the slider or the number box to simulate real survey conditions, and the diagram beneath redraws to match. The step in the curve near 9 km is the sonar's deep mode transition. One caveat: the day rate amortizes campaign lumps over the current mapping days, so a short campaign makes shallow water look slightly worse than a long one would.

How overlap is defined

Overlap is the pairwise overlap between adjacent survey lines: line spacing = swath × (1 − overlap). Entering 20% means each line's swath overlaps the next line by 20% of the swath width, which is the standard NOAA and hydrographic convention. Do not double the value for having a neighbor on each side; the shared strip at each line pair is counted once, and 20% here reproduces NOAA-style 20% coverage exactly. The planner's line plan, the exported depth-band rates, and the Cost Model's depth sweep all use this identical formula, and the KR-OER-19-01 calibration (achieved 4.04 km effective swath versus 3.96 km predicted at 20%) confirms the convention against a real contract survey.

Results

The hero cards show cost per km² on both bases; the rest cover total campaign, cost per day, and blended km² per day. When a planner scenario carried a rotation delta, a Rotation Premium card prices the extra days the endurance limit costs versus a vessel that never came home, at the campaign day rate. Note the premium runs slightly high by construction, since those extra days are transit and port weighted and cost a little less than the blended rate. When the scenario carried depth bands, a table shows dollars per km² per band as if the whole area sat at that depth, using the planner's calibrated rates.

Export

Open print report produces a print-ready page (use the browser's print to save a PDF) in the same visual language as the planner's Transit Range Report. It is built to be audited by someone who did not run the model: the survey area figure carried over from the planner, the campaign day breakdown with routing, weather margin, coverage realization, deployments and port calls when a planner scenario supplied them, a rate and quantity assumptions block listing speeds, hotel loads, derived fuel burn, operating days per year, XBT frequency or the manual probe count (whichever the line actually uses), deployments and port calls, and processing hours, a "what drives the cost" table ranking the largest lines by share, then every cost line with its driver, quantity, unit, count, rate range with units, totals and survey-only mark, an explicit list of what was excluded, the benchmark comparison with ratios, the depth band table and the depth cost table. The .xlsx contains Parameters, All-In Model, and Survey-Only Model sheets, plus a Depth Bands sheet when a scenario supplied bands. Quantities and totals are live formulas referencing the Parameters sheet, so anyone auditing the workbook can trace every number and change assumptions. The Parameters sheet carries a third column, Used by, naming the cost lines that reference each row; a parameter that only feeds the app, such as the hotel loads behind the fuel curve or the per-day XBT rate while the line is on a manual count, says so rather than sitting unexplained. Band rates and per-band dollar figures are written as values with their provenance noted, and the depth band table states its convention: day-driven lines and probe counts scale with each band's mapping days, lump sums and other manual quantities are held at campaign values, so band figures are not simply the day rate divided by the coverage rate. The steamed transit distance appears as its own parameter so transit fuel is traceable.

Where the $81 benchmark comes from

The KR-OER-19-01 benchmark is derived from the federal award record, not from a published rate. The delivery order is 1305M219FNCNJ0266, awarded to Fugro Pelagos, Inc. under indefinite delivery vehicle EA133C14CQ0032, with $1,992,492 obligated and a period of performance from August 2019 to August 2020, recorded as complete. Dividing that by the 31,900 square kilometres the NOAA mapping report states were mapped gives $62.46 per square kilometre in 2019 dollars. Multiplying by 1.30 for consumer price inflation between 2019 and 2026 gives approximately $81 per square kilometre in current dollars.

One caution remains. Inflation adjustment is a blunt instrument for marine services, whose costs move with fuel, vessel supply, and labour markets rather than with a consumer basket, so the adjusted figure carries more uncertainty than the arithmetic suggests. The unadjusted $62.46 is available as a second preset for comparisons run in the contract's own dollars, and the conclusion holds on either basis.

What the price covered matters as much as its size. Under the statement of work the contractor provided the vessel, crew, and systems for up to 40 calendar days, absorbed weather downtime, paid port and docking costs, ran crosslines and backscatter normalisation lines, and delivered processed data to bathymetric attributed grid standard. Transit to and from the region and mobilisation were excluded. A comparison drawn against this benchmark should therefore include weather and crossline allowances in the modelled mapping days and count port fees inside the survey-only basis, which is what the parity method below does.

Reading the printed cost lines

Each line in the report shows its quantity driver, the quantity, the unit, any crew or unit count, the rate range with its unit attached, both totals and the survey-only mark. Rates always carry their unit, so a daily rate cannot be mistaken for an annual one. Annual lines are shown as the campaign days against a prorated annual rate, for example 18 days against an annual rate divided by 150 operating days, which is how a licence or an insurance premium becomes a cost for one job. Itemised annual lines print their components underneath the parent. The depth sweep chart at the end of the report is marginal economics for a single depth at its own overlap setting and is independent of the campaign above, which took its line plan from the planner.

The KR-OER-19-01 parity method

The tool family was validated against this NOAA contract because its report publishes the answer key: 31,900 km² over 7,892 km of track, which is 4.04 km of achieved effective swath. The planner's EM302-class curve at Blake Plateau depths with 20% overlap predicts 3.96 km, a 2% match. The parity recipe: import the cleaned contract-area geometry, overlap 20%, XBTs 4 per mapping day, processing ratios 1.0 to 1.5, add about 4% to mapping days for crosslines and normalization lines, bake the weather factor into mapping days, set coverage realization in the planner to about 60% so the plan reflects what that contract actually achieved rather than the calm water curve, and tick Svy on for Port & Agency Fees and Crew Rotation Travel. Plan on the full cleaned coverage area but divide by the reported 31,900 when quoting dollars per km², which is deliberately conservative. Under that recipe the Ocean Mapper prices the same survey at roughly $4 to $7 per km² survey-only against the $81 benchmark, and $7 to $12 with capital fully loaded.

Sending a model out for review

The usual pairing is the planner's Transit Range Report for the time and the cost model's print report for the money, covering the same polygon. Between them a reviewer can check every number: the planner shows how the line plan and deployments produce the mapping days, and the cost report shows how those days and the entered rates produce the dollars. Any figure in the cost table can be reconstructed by multiplying quantity by count by rate, and the assumptions block gives the inputs behind each quantity.

Three things are worth flagging to a reviewer up front, because they are where a model of this kind is most often wrong. The fixed annual lines, insurance, maintenance reserve and the sonar service agreement, are the least certain and should be replaced with real quotes. The labour structure may imply more people than the persons aboard figure, which is coherent only if some support is shore based, so it is worth confirming. And the model shows base cost with no contingency, overhead or margin unless those rows are switched on, which matters before anyone compares it against a commercial price that includes all three.

Reading the numbers honestly

The default rates are placeholders chosen for structure. Labor sits on real market day rates; software reflects actual license costs (Qimera plus ArcGIS Pro prorated); the fixed lines (insurance, maintenance reserve, spares) likely run low for a new high-value hull and deserve real quotes before anything is presented externally. The robust finding is the ratio, not the digits: correcting every flagged rate to its pessimistic end moves the parity result from roughly 12 to 20 times cheaper to roughly 6 to 10 times, and the conclusion survives because a 23 meter vessel with two crew is not a 65 meter ship with twenty. These are planning estimates, not quotes and not survey deliverables. Check outputs against trusted tools before relying on them.
