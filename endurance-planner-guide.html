Endurance Planner, User Guide

Seirios Solutions · Toolbox · Overview, walkthrough, and extension reference
Part 1 · Overview

What this tool is

The Endurance Planner estimates where a crewed survey vessel can operate productively. A work area within fuel range may still be unusable if reaching it consumes most of the deployment. The tool turns that tradeoff into a picture: an interactive globe shaded by operational reach, a site checker for any point in the ocean, a survey planner that prices a drawn polygon in deployments and calendar days, and a one click report suitable for printing.

It is a single HTML file. Everything runs locally in the browser tab and nothing you load is ever uploaded. Three optional live map layers and the web fonts need the internet; the rest works without it. It opens with a representative configuration; the first thing to do is set the numbers to the vessel actually under evaluation.

The core idea

The model is deliberately simple so results can be checked by hand. The vessel transits at a set speed, so one transit day covers a fixed distance. Each deployment runs a fixed number of days port to port, less any weather margin held back. A minimum share of each deployment, 50% by default, must be spent on station for the deployment to count as productive. Whatever remains is the transit allowance, capped by fuel range. Every point in the ocean is then scored against its nearest ports along great circle tracks, and the globe is shaded with one of four outcomes:

Within the efficiency limit Multiport corridor Reachable, below minimum Out of reach

Within the efficiency limit means the point clears the productivity bar from at least one port. A multiport corridor clears it only by entering from one port and exiting to a different one. Reachable, below minimum means the vessel can get there and back inside one deployment but spends too much of it underway. Out of reach means no port is close enough even if the entire deployment were spent in transit. The stat cards above the globe report these shares for the world ocean and the United States Exclusive Economic Zone (US EEZ).

Quick start

  1. Open the file in any modern browser. Drag to rotate, scroll or pinch to zoom; port and vertex markers shrink as you zoom in so they stay readable. The Pacific, Atlantic, and Indian buttons fly to each basin.
  2. In the Vessel panel, enter transit speed, deployment length, weather margin, fuel range, and draft. The readout below states which constraint is binding, the crew calendar or fuel.
  3. In Efficiency, set the minimum on station share. The globe reshades as you type.
  4. Read the stat cards, then click any point of water for a site check, or draw a polygon for a full survey costing.
Part 2 · Walkthrough

Every control, what it does, and why it is included. Each choice encodes an operational judgment, and the judgment is stated so it can be questioned.

The Vessel panel

Transit speed (kn) sets how much distance one day of transit buys, and every reach calculation flows from it. Deployment length (days) is the port to port duration of one crew rotation. Weather / ops margin (days) is time held back from every deployment for weather, gear trouble, and everything else that is not mapping; it is subtracted before the transit allowance is computed. Fuel range (nm) caps total distance per deployment. Draft (m) filters the port list: a port must offer draft plus 0.5 m under keel clearance to stay active, and the line under the input reports live how many ports the current draft excludes, by name.

Why the binding constraint line existsBelow these inputs the tool states which limit actually governs reach at the current settings, the crew calendar or fuel. On a long range vessel with short rotations, fuel feels like the reach number but almost never is: a 6,000 nm tank supports roughly 25 days underway while a 10 day rotation grants 10. Making the tool state this explicitly prevents the most common misreading of the analysis.
Why margin defaults to zeroZero margin shows the calm water envelope, the best case, which is a reasonable baseline for a capability discussion. Raise the margin to 2 or 3 days before quoting any percentage. The exported report flags the zero margin case automatically.

The Efficiency panel

Minimum efficiency (%) is the share of a deployment that must be spent on station for the deployment to count as productive. The readout converts it to a concrete transit budget in days and nautical miles each way.

Why 50%, and why it is arguableHalf the deployment on station is a defensible floor for a mapping vessel; below that, most of the charter is spent in transit. The threshold is a judgment call, and different programs will defend different numbers. The slider exists precisely so the threshold can be interrogated live; the shape of the map changes far less than the percentages do, which is itself informative.
Why multiport corridors existA mapping campaign run as a lawnmower serpentine does not end where it started; it ends at the far side of the survey. Scoring every point on the assumption of returning to the departure port would therefore misprice exactly the operations this vessel will run, and large stretches of remote ocean would falsely read as unreachable when in fact they are workable by entering from one port and exiting to another. The corridor category scores that pattern honestly: same transit budget, split between two different ports. It is shown as its own color because corridor water requires campaign style planning rather than out and back runs.

Shade water below minimum toggles the red and near black wash. Transit day rings draws rings of constant round trip transit time around the port network, useful for briefing audiences who think in days rather than colors.

The Survey production panel

This panel carries the single most consequential setting in the tool: whether swath comes from a fixed number or from the sonar. Manual swath holds one width everywhere regardless of depth, which is fine for a quick sketch and wrong for any real survey where the depth varies. From depth (sonar curve) takes the swath from the selected sonar's coverage curve at the actual depth under the polygon, which is what makes the mapping days trustworthy. The panel shows a standing amber notice whenever manual mode is active, and it disappears once the sonar curve is selected. A collapsible four step summary sits at the top of the panel, and every input label carries a question mark; click it and a short explanation opens beneath the field.

Survey speed (kn) is line running speed, typically slower than transit speed, and drives the coverage rate. Line turn (min) is the cost of each end of line turn; the report shows what share of survey time turns consume. Port turnaround (d) is time between deployments, crew change, fuel, provisions, and it counts against calendar time but not against any single deployment.

Swath input has two modes. Manual swath takes a width in kilometers directly, useful for quick what ifs or a sonar the tool does not know. From depth (sonar curve) computes achievable swath from water depth using digitized performance curves for the Kongsberg EM124 2°×2° and EM304 MKII 1°×2°, calibrated against operator supplied data points from the manufacturer runtime graphs. Max angle caps the swath angle per side regardless of what the curve offers, reflecting the outer beam quality limits a chief surveyor actually enforces.

Why two sonars, and why the comparison mattersThe two curves make the frequency argument computable. At 6,000 m the 12 kHz EM124 achieves roughly 25 km of swath where the 30 kHz EM304 achieves 11, because two way absorption attenuates higher frequencies severely at depth. Flip the sonar dropdown on any deep survey polygon and the deployment count roughly doubles; the exported report computes this comparison automatically.

Depth source chooses what depth feeds the curve. Polygon bathymetry, the default, samples a global depth grid embedded in the tool (ETOPO 2022 bedrock, decimated to 0.25 degree block means) inside the drawn polygon and computes line spacing separately for each 250 m depth band. Static average uses a single typed depth everywhere, respected without override, and doubles as the fallback when no polygon exists. The hint line under the selector always states which source is actually in effect.

Why per band spacing instead of an average depthLine spacing scales with depth, so shallow water is disproportionately expensive: 10,000 km² of 500 m water needs several times the line distance of the same area at 5,000 m. Averaging depth first hides that, and a polygon straddling a shelf edge would be badly underpriced. Weighting each depth band by its share of the polygon area corrects this, and it is why the report can flag, for example, that 56% of a polygon shallower than 1,000 m is what drives the spacing down.

The Layers panel

GEBCO basemap, dim unmapped seafloor renders the global grid with unmapped water dimmed, so measured coverage glows against the gaps. NCEI multibeam mosaic overlays actual multibeam tracklines. US bathymetric gap analysis overlays the federal gap product for US waters. All three are live layers with graceful offline fallback, and the export records each layer vintage so a report is traceable to its data date. US EEZ draws the boundary from Marine Regions EEZ v12.

Why the dim unmapped view is the defaultThe unmapped seafloor is the product this whole analysis serves. Rendering mapped water bright and unmapped water dark shows at a glance how little of any basin has been surveyed.

Base imagery resolution chooses how much detail the globe requests from the live services: Auto picks 8K where the graphics hardware and memory allow it and falls back to 4K otherwise, Standard 4K forces the lighter option, and High 8K forces the sharper one. The choice is remembered and takes effect after a reload. At 8K each layer is fetched as four tiles and assembled locally, roughly 5 km per pixel at the equator; this is planning map resolution either way, and inspecting a specific survey area up close remains a job for the NCEI Bathymetric Data Viewer.

The Ports panel: how the list was decided

The port list is the backbone of every number in the tool, so its provenance deserves a plain statement. It contains 120 entries in two tiers. Tier 1, 79 ports, are primary support hubs: established fuel, provisioning, customs, and crew air access, the ports a program would base on without hesitation, with coverage audited basin by basin: the Mediterranean from Gibraltar to Limassol, the South American Atlantic from Port of Spain to Rio Grande, the Alaska panhandle through Seward and Juneau, the Indian Ocean rim from Durban to Fujairah, northern Europe from Southampton to Bergen, and Southeast Asia through Jakarta and Busan. The Tier 2 set was additionally cross checked against expedition cruise itineraries (Lindblad National Geographic, Viking, Seabourn), whose turnaround ports, Kangerlussuaq, Funchal, Rarotonga, Broome, and similar, are remote harbors with proven fuel, provisioning, and crew air access. The same cross check enforces a distinction the list is built on: a famous stop is not a support port. South Georgia, Easter Island, and Tristan da Cunha appear on expedition itineraries but not in this list; even 22 day expedition voyages run them as round trips from Ushuaia. An anchorage with no fuel and no airstrip cannot end a deployment, and modeling it as a port would falsely extend reach. Tier 2, 41 ports marked T2, are smaller or regional options that close real coverage gaps, Puerto Quetzal and Caldera on the Central American Pacific, Cartagena and Kingston in the western Caribbean, Taiohae and Baltra bridging the equatorial Pacific, Stanley and Puerto Madryn opening the far South Atlantic, and similar, but carry limited services, anchorage fueling, or permit requirements. A single checkbox strips the model back to Tier 1 for conservative scenarios, and the difference between the two settings is itself a finding: including Tier 2 ports adds roughly six percentage points of reachable ocean worldwide, and almost nothing inside the US EEZ, where port availability was never the constraint.

Some omissions are deliberate. Russian ports are excluded as currently impractical, Lagos on security grounds, restricted islands such as Midway and Diego Garcia on access grounds, the Red Sea corridor on security grounds, and the Black Sea entirely, since transit and access constraints there make planning figures meaningless. And some emptiness is real: the southeast Pacific between French Polynesia and Chile, the mid Atlantic between the Azores and Bermuda, and the central Arctic basin are genuine port deserts, places no port list edit changes; longer deployments are the only lever there.

Why depths are flagged as estimatesEach port depth is a typed in planning estimate, not a value pulled from any dataset, and the panel says so explicitly. They range from well established figures at major hubs to judgment calls at small anchorages, and harbors silt and get dredged. For a shallow draft vessel none of this matters, no port in the list excludes a 2.5 m boat, but the moment a depth becomes decision relevant, verify it against current sailing directions and encode the verified figure as a custom port.

Custom ports: the main extension point

The Add your own port form takes a name, position, and depth. The position can be typed, or picked directly: press Pick on globe, click the port location on the map, coasts and land included, and the coordinates fill in automatically. Each field validates with explicit feedback, and the port joins the model instantly: it joins the reach shading, the site check rankings, the survey routing, and the report map, and it appears at the top of the port list with a remove button. Custom ports persist in the browser where storage allows. This is the intended mechanism for the common case where an organization already knows its candidate piers, enter the options under consideration, uncheck everything else, and every figure in the tool reflects only those ports.

Site check

Click any point of open water, or type a position into the coordinate box at the top of the panel. A rust colored marker drops, larger than the deep red port dots so the two are never confused, and route lines fan to the three nearest ports. The panel ranks the six nearest active ports by one way distance, transit days, round trip days, days remaining on station, and the resulting efficiency. The line above the table shows the best multiport option, entering from one port and exiting to another, with its verdict and the repositioning cost versus returning. Fuel range violations are flagged in red.

Measure

Press Measure and click points on the globe to build a path; each click adds a waypoint and the legs draw as dashed gold great circles. Waypoints can also be typed into the coordinate box on the readout card, so a path can start from an exact known position rather than a click. A floating readout card on the globe keeps a running total in nautical miles, kilometers, and transit days at the current speed. From the third point on, a fainter dashed line also shows the direct great circle from the first point to the last, and the readout states both figures side by side: the path total, and what the path adds over the straight line. Pressing Measure again finishes and keeps the path on screen for reference; pressing it once more starts fresh, and Clear removes it.

Why path and straight line togetherRouting questions are usually comparisons, a detour around a cape or via a bunker stop against the direct line. Showing the path cost and the straight line in the same card answers the comparison directly. Legs run straight through everything, land included; the tool measures geometry only.

Coordinate entry

Every place the tool takes a clicked position also takes a typed one: the site check panel, the measure card, and the survey area card each carry a coordinate box. The parser accepts decimal degrees with signs or hemisphere letters (-24.91, -38.51 or 24.91S 38.51W or S24.91 W38.51), degrees and decimal minutes (24 54.6 S), and full degrees minutes seconds, in either latitude or longitude order when letters are present. Longitudes given in the 0 to 360 convention wrap correctly, which matters when working across the dateline. Multiple pairs separated by line breaks or semicolons are accepted in one entry, and anything unreadable is reported back without discarding the readable pairs.

The survey report

Draw area, click at least three vertices on the water, then Finish, double click, or right click. A floating card on the globe keeps a running vertex count and area total while you draw, and it states the assumptions the report will price with: the sonar curve or manual swath, the depth source in effect, and the overlap. Check that line before quoting a number; a setting carried over from the last scenario is the easiest way to get a plausible but wrong figure. Vertices can be typed or pasted into the card's coordinate box, one pair per line or separated by semicolons, which is the intended path when a survey area arrives as a defined boundary: paste the corner list from the document, press Finish, and the polygon is exact rather than eyeballed. The report prices the polygon top to bottom: spherical area with land automatically detected and excluded; the depth distribution found inside the polygon, median, range, and a flag when shallow water drives cost; the line plan with total distance and the spacing that produced it; mapping time including turns; the ports the routing actually chose, shown on an embedded map with a graticule and scale bar, nearby unused ports for context, and dashed transit connectors labeled with the one way distance from the polygon to each used port. The map preserves true geographic proportions at every frame shape, widening its window when needed rather than distorting, so the polygon on paper has the same shape as the polygon on the globe; deployments and calendar time as the headline badge; and the endurance cost box: the same survey with unlimited endurance, and what the rotation adds. A comparison hint also shows what single port operation would have cost versus the end of line routing the model uses.

Why the routing enters and exits at different portsThe serpentine ends each deployment wherever the clock runs out, and the model sends the vessel to the nearest port from that spot, then resumes from the nearest port to the resume point. This mirrors how an operator would run a long campaign, and applies the same logic as the multiport corridor category on the globe.

The export report

Add view captures the current globe framing, up to six views; port names are composited into every capture regardless of zoom level, even when the live screen hides them to reduce clutter. Export report opens a print ready document written for a reader who has never seen the app: The question states the problem in plain language; How the model works defines the four categories in prose before any percentage appears, then the settings table; What that means for reach gives the world and US EEZ results; The lever: deployment length holds every vessel number fixed and sweeps rotation length, with the current setting highlighted and a bold one sentence reading of the table beneath it; and, when a polygon is drawn, The survey scenario prices the drawn area with its rotation versus unlimited endurance comparison and the automatic alternate sonar line. Data vintages, sources, and caveats close it, with the Seirios logo at the bottom. Print / Save as PDF in the report window produces the shareable file.

Part 3 · Extending the tool

Adding ports permanently

Custom ports cover day to day use. To bake ports into the file itself, open the HTML in a text editor, find const PORTS = [, and add a line in the same format:

["Name", "Country", latitude, longitude, "Category", depth_m, "note", tier]

Category is one of "US", "US Territory", or "International" and controls which list group the port appears under. Depth is meters alongside or controlling. The note appears as a tooltip and an asterisk. Tier is 1 or 2; omit it for Tier 1. Save the file and reload; the port participates in everything.

Refreshing the depth grid

The embedded bathymetry comes from ETOPO 2022, decimated with the companion Seirios Grid Decimator tool, which block averages any large GeoTIFF down to a planning grid entirely in the browser. To refresh or substitute the grid, run a new GeoTIFF through the decimator at the 0.25 degree preset and supply the compact output for re embedding.

Adding a sonar

Sonar performance lives in the file as a table of achievable swath angle in degrees per side versus depth in meters, digitized from a manufacturer runtime coverage graph. Adding a system means adding one such table and one entry naming it, after which it appears in the Sonar dropdown and in the report comparisons. Calibrate the table against known data points, the existing curves reproduce their reference points to within a few meters of swath, and record the provenance.

Importing a survey footprint (GeoJSON)

The Import GeoJSON button in the map toolbar loads a polygon from a file instead of drawing it. It accepts a FeatureCollection, a single Feature, or a bare geometry, with Polygon or MultiPolygon shapes, and expects plain longitude and latitude degrees (EPSG:4326). If you export from QGIS, set the CRS to EPSG:4326 in the Save Features As dialog; Pseudo-Mercator coordinates will import as garbage.

When a file holds more than one area, a banner at the top of the survey report names every area with its size, marks which one the report covers, and carries two buttons: one adds the shown area to the campaign and scrolls the campaign panel into view, the other switches to the next area. That is the intended path for a footprint with separate survey boxes. Importing flies the globe to the polygon and frames it, and the survey report runs immediately, exactly as if the area had been drawn by hand. Rings smaller than half a percent of the largest are dropped as slivers, which quietly removes patch test and calibration lines from a survey footprint. Multi-part files load the largest ring first. When more parts exist, a Showing area 1 of N, switch button appears beside the import button and cycles through them, and a banner at the top of the survey report names every area with its size and marks which one the report covers, so a footprint holding two contract areas can be planned one area at a time. Rings are simplified to at most 120 vertices with Douglas-Peucker before planning; on a real NCEI survey footprint this cost 0.04% of area. Tiny slivers under 0.01 km² are dropped automatically, which quietly removes patch test and normalization lines.

Two limits to know. Interior holes in a footprint (coverage gaps) are ignored, so a gappy footprint reads slightly large. Rings crossing the antimeridian are not handled; the tool warns and the area will be wrong.

One practical note on NCEI multibeam footprints: they are generalized envelopes of everything the sonar touched, including transit legs run with the sonar on. If you want the contract survey areas rather than the as-covered envelope, clean the geometry first (a morphological opening of about 3 km in a projected CRS removes ribbons and legs while keeping the area cores), or draw the areas directly.

Sending a scenario to the Cost Model

The Send to Cost Model button in the map toolbar packages the current area report as a JSON scenario file (also copied to the clipboard) that the Survey Vessel Cost Model imports directly. This is the intended workflow: plan here first, price there second. The planner owns time; the cost model owns dollars.

The scenario carries the ocean area, one-way distance to the nearest port, mapping and transit days as overrides, port turnaround days, weather margin days, speeds, overlap, endurance, deployments, and the rotation delta (calendar days versus a vessel with unlimited endurance). It also carries the survey report's own line plan figure and the area outline as GeoJSON, so the cost model's print report can show the same map. It also carries calibrated depth bands: with depth mode on, the polygon's 250 m ETOPO histogram is aggregated into up to seven bands, each with its area share, weighted depth, and a km² per day rate from the active sonar's swath curve, your max angle, and overlap. With depth mode off it falls back to one blended band at area divided by mapping days.

Multi-area campaigns use the CAMPAIGN panel under the survey report. Plan an area (drawn or imported), press Add shown area, switch to the next area and add it too. The panel keeps a running total: combined area, mapping days, transits, port days, deployments, and calendar time, with one port turnaround added at each joint between areas, and the campaign section of the report states the port call count. Line kilometres are summed over 250 m depth bands, so shallow ground is planned at tighter spacing and deep ground at wider spacing; the drawn plan and the turn count use a single area-weighted spacing and supply the bearing and the picture only. Because banded spacing is only achievable when lines run along the isobaths, the area report also states what the same polygon would cost in line kilometres and mapping days if lines ran across the slope, where the shallowest spacing has to cover everything. On a steep polygon that difference can exceed 100%, so check the plan bearing against the isobath direction before relying on the shorter figure. Once each area has been planned, the multi-area banner reports ocean area with land cells excluded, the same figure the campaign and the cost model use. A routing selector appears once a campaign holds more than one area. Separate mobilisations, the default, treats each area as its own job: a round trip from its own nearest port, with a port turnaround between areas, and no steaming between the two working ports. Chained single cruise routes one continuous cruise instead, out to the first area, directly between areas on a great circle, and home from the last, with no port call in between; it assumes endurance and crewing allow the areas to be worked back to back, and it recomputes deployments across the whole campaign rather than summing them per area. The exported report states which routing was used and, when chained, how many nautical miles were steamed between areas. Send campaign to Cost Model exports one combined scenario, with depth bands merged area-weighted across the areas, and each area's line plan figure travelling with it.

The exported report changes shape once a campaign exists. The campaign totals lead: a table of every area with its size, mapping days, transit, port days and deployments, a row for the port turnaround added between each pair of areas, the campaign total, the calendar time badge and the endurance cost across the whole job. The detailed walkthrough that follows covers one area only and is retitled to say so, for example "Area detail: Area 1 (1 of 2 shown in full)", so the method and the rotation comparison are clearly about that area rather than the campaign. Line plan figures appear once each: the area carried through the detail section keeps its map there, and the other areas' maps sit in the campaign section. The combination is slightly conservative: a real campaign may chain the last partial deployment of one area straight into the next, which the sum does not credit.

Inside the cost model, the planner's day counts take charge: the model's own depth estimator stands down, transit fuel is computed from the total steamed distance (which covers multiport deployments correctly), and the weather margin days are folded into port days so day-counted costs stay honest. The rotation delta becomes a priced Rotation Premium card. The scenario also carries the deployment count, a port call count, the routing choice, the weather margin and the coverage realization setting, so the cost report can state how the days were planned and price crew rotation travel and port fees from the same campaign structure. Port calls follow one convention: each deployment starts and ends alongside, consecutive deployments worked from the same port share the call between them, and separate mobilisations from different ports do not, so a chained cruise is deployments plus one while separate jobs sum each area's deployments plus one. The count is editable in the cost model. If you later change speeds or days in the cost model, re-export from the planner rather than editing around the import.

Coverage realization

The sonar coverage curves are manufacturer figures: what the system can achieve in good conditions with a well behaved water column. Real surveys do not hold that. Sea state, sound speed structure, quality driven angle and speed limits, and reruns of failed lines all cut into it. The Coverage realization input in the Survey production panel is the share of the curve actually expected at sea, and it multiplies the swath after the curve and the overlap, so it flows through line spacing, mapping days, deployments and the depth bands exported to the Cost Model.

Three anchors are worth remembering. At 100% the plan is the calm water ideal, useful as a best case but not as a schedule. 85%, the default, is a reasonable working assumption. The Fugro KR-OER-19-01 contract, which published both its area and its track distance, realized about 60% of the curve for an EM302 on the Blake Plateau. For a like for like comparison against a contracted survey, set realization to what that contract achieved rather than planning the ideal and comparing it to somebody else's reality. The setting is recorded in the exported report so the assumption travels with the document.

How overlap is defined

Overlap is the pairwise overlap between adjacent survey lines: line spacing = swath × (1 − overlap). Entering 20% means each line's swath overlaps the next line by 20% of the swath width, which is the standard NOAA and hydrographic convention. Do not double the value for having a neighbor on each side; the shared strip at each line pair is counted once, and 20% here reproduces NOAA-style 20% coverage exactly. The planner's line plan, the exported depth-band rates, and the Cost Model's depth sweep all use this identical formula, and the KR-OER-19-01 calibration (achieved 4.04 km effective swath versus 3.96 km predicted at 20%) confirms the convention against a real contract survey.

Reading the numbers honestly

These are planning estimates, not operational guidance. Distances are great circle with no routing around land, and polar reach ignores sea ice and seasonal access. With the weather margin at zero the picture is a calm water envelope; test sensitivity before quoting figures. Port depths are typed in planning estimates without per port citations. The embedded bathymetry is decimated to 0.25 degree block means, suitable for depth distributions and time planning; it cannot resolve features smaller than about 30 km and is not survey grade. The line planner works on a flat local plane, coarse for polygons spanning more than about 15 degrees. Swath curves are digitized from manufacturer runtime graphs; achievable coverage varies with conditions and settings. The US EEZ boundary is simplified and not for legal use.
